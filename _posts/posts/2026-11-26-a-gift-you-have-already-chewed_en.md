Some people I have worked for had a habit of giving books. You would come out of a meeting with a paperback in your hand and a sentence attached to it: *I think this one is for you.* The book itself cost less than lunch. The sentence was the expensive part. It meant somebody had read the thing, thought about you, and decided the two belonged together.

A while ago I caught myself doing the same thing with something that is not a book. I had been using a small AI skill of my own — an adversarial analyst I named, with some self-indulgence, after Jia Xu, the coldest strategist in the Three Kingdoms. Its job is to take whatever plan I am pleased with and look for the place where it quietly falls apart. I had grown fond of it, the way one grows fond of a colleague who is always slightly unpleasant and always slightly right. And I thought: a friend of mine could use this. So I packaged it up, wrote a short note, and handed it over.

Then I started wondering what exactly I had given.

## From "read this" to "use this"

The shape of the gift has changed. A book says *read this, and you may come to think the way I do.* A skill says something closer to *here is the way I think about one kind of problem — you can put it to work this afternoon.* The first is an invitation to a long digestion. The second is a tool that arrives already digested.

That is where its value comes from, and it is also where my unease comes from. What made the skill worth giving was not the file. Anyone can copy a file. What made it worth giving was the months of using it, tuning the questions it asks, noticing where it was lazy, telling it to be harsher here and quieter there. In other words, the gift is something I have already chewed. Handing someone a tool you have never used yourself is forwarding a link. Handing them one you have argued with for a season is something else.

I suspect this will matter more, not less. If the number of available agents keeps growing the way it seems to — and that is a guess, not a measurement — the scarce thing will not be the agents. It will be knowing which one fits *this* person, doing *this* work, at *this* point in their life. That knowledge has always been the real content of a good book recommendation. It just used to come wrapped in paper.

## The trouble with things that have no price

Here is the part I have not resolved. A book has a price on the back. It is recognisably a gift: you can wrap it, the other person can see roughly what it cost you, and the social machinery of thanks runs smoothly. A homemade skill has no price on the back. The hours that went into it are invisible. To the person receiving it, it may look like a text file.

So it is entirely possible that the more useful the gift becomes, the less it looks like a gift. That is a strange trade, and I am not sure I like it.

There are some obvious objections to my worry, and I think they are fair. A handwritten letter has no price either, and nobody mistakes it for nothing. The question may not be whether the gift has a price, but whether the care that went into choosing and fitting it can be seen. Those are different things — use, relationship, and the small ceremony of recognisable giving — and a skill can score high on one and low on another.

And there is a second objection, which is less flattering to me. A gift is not only what it gives; it is also what it asks. A book asks for a few evenings. A skill may ask the recipient to understand terms they have never heard, install things, and then argue with someone else's habits until they become their own. A gift that comes with a setup guide is halfway to being an assignment. And a tool for critical thinking has its own risk of being misread: it can say *I thought of you*, or it can say *I think you need help thinking*. I do not know which my friend heard, and I have decided not to guess.

## What a skill does not carry

One more honest limit. A skill passes on a method and a little of the experience behind it. It does not pass on the judgement of the person who built it, the context they work in, or the long, boring history of using it badly before using it well. My friend will have to rebuild some of that. Which is, come to think of it, exactly what happens with books. Nobody ever finished a good book and found the author's judgement installed.

Perhaps that is the right way to see it. The gift is not the finished thinking. It is a starting point someone cared enough to choose for you, and the chewing that matters most is still the chewing you do yourself.

What I keep coming back to is the question underneath all of this: when a gift no longer has a clear price, how do we make the other person see the understanding inside it? I suspect the answer is unglamorous. Write the sentence. *I think this one is for you, and here is why.* It was always the expensive part.
