I sent an email about something I had decided was important. The inbox chimed back within seconds, and for one absurd moment I felt attended to.

It was an automatic reply. A colleague was on holiday and would not be reading email until the middle of August. What stopped me was not the absence but the inventory that came with it. She listed what she would be doing instead: seeing family and friends, going somewhere quiet where opening the door puts you straight into nature, reading books unrelated to work, and not setting an alarm in the morning.

I read the line about the alarm three times.

## The genre of the apologetic absence

I have written these messages myself. Mine go something like: *I am checking email infrequently at the moment; if your matter is urgent, please…* — and that trailing ellipsis is doing an enormous amount of cowardly work. Translated honestly it says: I will in fact still be reading. If you push, I will answer. I could not bring myself to shut the door, so I left it open a crack and hoped nobody would feel the draft.

Hers had no crack. She was not notifying me of an absence; she was telling me what kind of days she was having, down to the alarm clock. There is a difference between *I am unavailable* and *I am doing something else*, and it is roughly the difference between an apology and a life.

The best line came at the end. Urgent matters could go to the office, she wrote, but please note that they too are enjoying their well-earned rest, so replies may be slow.

Meaning: there is no fast lane here.

## The urgency I built myself

My first reaction was mild panic, because the matter sat near the top of my list. By the evening it had slid down several places on its own, without intervention from anyone. Nothing about the world had changed. What changed was that I stopped supplying it with urgency.

This is the part I find funny, in the way things are funny when they are about you. I had placed the matter in a position that demanded an immediate answer, and then experienced the demand as though it had arrived from outside, like weather. Nobody put it there. I did. The deadline was a piece of furniture I dragged into the room myself and then complained about tripping over.

Academic life runs this trick at scale. Very little in it is genuinely time-critical — nobody bleeds out because a supervision meeting slips a week — and yet the ambient pressure never lets up, which strongly suggests the pressure is not coming from the work. It is coming from a tacit agreement among the people doing the work that we will all behave as though it were. An autoreply that declines the agreement is therefore not a small administrative act. It is a defection. Defection is how these agreements end.

## What I actually did about it

Nothing, so far. I still cannot write that message. My drafts keep sprouting escape hatches. Some part of me is convinced that unavailability is a claim I have not yet earned the standing to make, and that the correct posture for a junior person is to be permanently reachable. This is probably false. It is certainly convenient for everyone except me.

But I saved hers. It sits in a folder, and I intend to plagiarize it shamelessly the first summer I am brave enough — not the wording, the posture. The willingness to say what you are doing rather than only what you are not doing. To let the alarm clock stay off. To trust that anything genuinely important will still be important in two weeks, which, in my experience, is a test almost nothing passes.
