Last week I spent ten minutes in front of a room of university teachers, opening a workshop on keeping pedagogical and ethical judgement human in the era of generative AI. A colleague took the ethical half; I took the pedagogical one. Ten minutes is not long enough to say anything clever, which turned out to be useful, because it forced me to say only the things I still believed after two years of watching the tools change underneath me every few months.

I started with two questions that need nothing but hands. First: who has recently used AI to design teaching or prepare a course? Second: who has a clear rule for when they would *not* use it? The gap between those two questions is the whole subject. We have become fluent users long before we became deliberate ones.

## A small experiment you can run on yourself

Before saying anything about principles, I asked everyone to do something. Imagine a university tutorial group in the year 2045. Write down three things you see. Forty-five seconds. Draw a line.

Then I put up a rough sketch an AI had made from the same prompt — a round table, a holographic heart floating above it, augmented-reality glasses, a friendly little robot by a touchscreen wall — and asked for three *more* ideas below the line, as different as possible from everything seen so far.

I will not report what the room did, because a room is not a study. But the study exists. At CHI 2024, Wadinambiarachchi and colleagues ran a version of this with sixty participants: people who generated ideas with an AI image generator fixated on its first examples and produced fewer ideas, with less variety and less originality, than people working without it. Once you have seen a draft, it is very hard to unsee it. And if your own first round already contained the hologram and the robot, notice that too. The AI's sketch is often just the average of our shared clichés, delivered with confidence.

That was the point of the exercise. Everything else I said hung from it.

## Four things that survived every update

The tools have changed so much that any demonstration I could have shown would probably be out of date by next month. What has not changed is a short list.

**Think first, then prompt.** Decide the learning goals and the shape of the session yourself, then give the machine bounded tasks. Whoever speaks first sets the frame. Let it be you.

**Use AI to deepen your understanding, not to skip it.** This matters most when you design outside your own expertise — which, in university teaching, happens more than anyone admits. When I once had to design an activity for a course in a field I was not trained in, I did not ask the AI to write anything. I explained the course to it in my own words and asked it to correct me. It started asking me questions back: where are these students in their training, what do they already know? Explaining it myself had two side effects. Nothing confidential went into the tool. And by the end I could argue with the course coordinator as a colleague, instead of handing over a draft I could not defend. That understanding is also what later lets you notice when an answer is wrong.

**AI drafts, humans validate.** However polished the output looks, check whether it is accurate, whether it serves the objectives, whether it works in *your* classroom. In the well-known experiment with 758 consultants by Dell'Acqua and colleagues, AI made work faster and better on tasks inside its capabilities — and on a task just outside them, people using it were nineteen percentage points less likely to get it right. The boundary does not show in the output. Polish is not evidence.

**Responsibility does not scale with automation.** If the machine writes a case, an expert makes a few edits, and an error reaches students, it is still our course and our name. Madeleine Elish calls this the moral crumple zone: when automated systems fail, the blame lands on the nearest human. In teaching, the nearest human is us.

## One sentence to take home

If I had to compress the ten minutes into one line, it would be this: AI amplifies clarity; it does not create it. A teacher who knows what the session is for will get a better session out of the machine. A teacher who does not will get a very confident average.

That is the pedagogical lens. A design can pass it and still raise questions about bias, harm, and who bears the cost — which is why the workshop had a second half, and why I was glad not to be the one giving it alone.
